The Airport Goodbye

Shared by Dominic

  • December 2013
  • Romance scam
  • Philippines

I met a woman online in 2013. We talked for months and had video calls, so I felt like I knew her. In December 2013, I flew to the Philippines to meet her in person.

From the moment I arrived, everything was about money. She had trips planned and expected me to pay not just for her, but for her extended family too. When I pushed back, the answer was basically “you’re American, you’re rich.” I refused. I paid for my own food and nothing more.

I broke up with her at the airport before flying home.

A few years later, around 2016, she contacted me out of nowhere. She said her brother had died and sent me a list of funeral expenses. She never directly asked for money, but the message was clear. I declined. I never heard from her again.

Looking back, there were signs I missed. There were always stories about everyone being sick, houses with holes in their roofs, one emergency after another. I believe her brother is still alive.

Warning for others

“If someone you’ve never met in person starts planning how you’ll spend your money, walk away. Video calls don’t mean you know someone. And a sad story years later is often just another try.”

Know the pattern

Common scam types.

The details change. The pressure, secrecy, and requests for money often repeat. These categories help readers compare the behavior—not label people.

01

Romance

Affection or a claimed relationship is used to build trust before requests for money begin.

02

Land or property

A purchase, title, lease, or construction arrangement is misrepresented or never completed.

03

Business partner

An investment or partnership depends on promises that do not match how the money is used.

04

Family emergency

An urgent medical, travel, funeral, or family crisis becomes the reason to send money quickly.

05

Fake visa or agent

Someone claims fees, documents, or payments are required for travel, legal help, or government processing.

06

Catfishing

A false identity, stolen profile, or invented personal history is used to create trust.

Pause before paying

Red flags worth slowing down for.

No single warning sign proves a scam. A pattern of urgency, secrecy, inconsistent details, and repeated requests is a reason to step back and verify.

Warning signs

  • Urgency replaces proofYou are pushed to act now before documents, identities, or details can be checked.
  • The story keeps changingDates, names, amounts, or reasons shift when you ask direct questions.
  • Requests must stay secretYou are discouraged from talking to family, friends, a lawyer, or another trusted person.
  • One payment becomes manyEvery transfer uncovers a new fee, emergency, delay, or final step.

Safer next steps

  • Slow the decision downA real need can withstand a reasonable pause while you check the facts.
  • Verify independentlyUse contact details and records you found yourself—not only what the other person sent.
  • Ask someone outside itShow the full situation to a trusted person who is not emotionally or financially involved.
  • Keep recordsSave messages, receipts, agreements, dates, and payment details for your own report.
